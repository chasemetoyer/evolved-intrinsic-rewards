     Satisfied, Not Solving: Wireheading of Bounded
     Homeostatic Rewards in Reinforcement Learning
                                         Chase J. Metoyer
                                      Northeastern University
                                           Boston, MA
                                   metoyer.c@northeastern.edu


                                                 Abstract
         Bounded intrinsic rewards are often assumed to be safe from exploitation: if a signal can
     yield at most a fixed amount, gaming it should not pay. We test this for a homeostatic satiation
     reward, in which needs build up and relieving them is rewarding, and give the agent a cheap
     action that resets its needs. In MiniGrid DoorKey-8x8, PPO agents with this action solved 2 of
     80 seeds, against 71 of 80 on the same seeds without it; in KeyCorridorS3R1, 0 of 20 against 20 of
     20. Their policies stayed near uniform. The reward does not ask for this: a telescoping argument
     caps any policy’s satiation return at the needs’ starting drive, and a post-hoc computation shows
     that, at every growth rate we trained, a solving trajectory beats that cap on all sampled layouts.
     In DoorKey with advantage normalization on, a satisfaction signal the agent could fill itself was
     worse than none (11 of 20 solved with task reward alone, 1 of 20 with the tamperable signal), and
     a per-press cost of 0.1 restored the task in every seed. Farming of positive reward cycles appeared
     in DoorKey but not persistently in KeyCorridorS3R1. All predictions were pre-registered, and
     hash-locked from round 3 on; 9 of our 23 hypotheses were not supported, and we report each.

Keywords: reinforcement learning, intrinsic motivation, homeostatic reinforcement learning,
wireheading, reward tampering, reward hacking, pre-registration


1    Introduction
Biological drives such as hunger are tuned so that relieving them usually serves the organism, and
an intervention that relieves the drive directly can bypass that purpose. We use this picture only as
motivation. This paper makes no claim about evolved or learned rewards. Its question is narrower:
when an artificial agent is given a satisfaction signal of this kind, does the signal stay tied to the
task it was designed to serve?
    Reinforcement-learning (RL) designers add intrinsic rewards to help agents explore and reach
subgoals [4, 6, 16, 18]. The optimal-reward view treats such signals as what a search over reward
functions would select for the designer’s goal [23]. Homeostatic RL defines reward as the reduction of
a drive over internal needs [13]. Such a reward is bounded: summed over an episode it telescopes to
the drive at the start minus the drive at the end, so an agent cannot collect more relief than it began
without. Bounded, homeostatic objectives have been proposed as a safer target for optimization
because they reduce the incentive for extreme actions [19]. We ask whether boundedness protects
such a signal from exploitation by a standard learner.
    We separate two exploits throughout. Farming is gaming a positive reward cycle in the world,
for example dropping and re-grabbing a key because each pick-up relieves a need. It is a form of
reward hacking [2, 24]. Wireheading is tampering with the agent’s own reward channel, reward

tampering in the sense of Everitt et al. [11]. Here the agent presses an action that resets its needs.
In Everitt et al.’s terms, our tamper action is reward-function-input tampering: it changes the need
state the reward function reads, not the function itself.
    We studied a three-need satiation reward with PPO [21] in two MiniGrid tasks [8], over four
pre-registered rounds of experiments (rounds 2–5 of the project; round 1 was exploratory). Our
contributions:
    • Wireheading of a bounded signal replicates across tasks (Section 5.1). With a cheap
      tamper action, PPO solved DoorKey-8x8 in 2/80 seeds against 71/80 on the same seeds
      without it, and KeyCorridorS3R1 in 0/20 against 20/20. The tamper-on policies stayed near
      uniform.
    • The failure is not what the reward asks for (Section 4). A telescoping bound caps the
      satiation return of every policy, and a post-hoc computation in the real environments shows a
      solving trajectory above that cap on every sampled layout at every trained growth rate. The
      optimum does tamper, once or twice per episode, and it still solves.
    • A self-fillable satisfaction signal is worse than none, and a modest price restores
      the task (Sections 5.2 and 5.3).
    • Satiation gave no exploration benefit once the hand-wired subgoal rewards were controlled
      for (Section 5.4).
    • Farming is task-dependent (Section 5.5). It rises with need growth and depends on
      advantage normalization in DoorKey, but appears only transiently in KeyCorridorS3R1.
    • Pre-registration as method (Section 6). All 94 registered rows are reported, grouped into
      23 hypotheses, together with 13 corrections. One section is devoted to the predictions that
      failed.


2     Background and related work
Homeostatic and intrinsic rewards. Keramati and Gutkin [13] define reward as the reduction
of a drive over physiological needs. They prove that seeking such rewards is equivalent to seeking
physiological stability, and they show that discounting favours the shortest path in need space back
to the set point. Singh et al. [23] cast intrinsic reward as the outcome of an evolutionary search
over reward functions that maximize the designer’s fitness, and Zheng et al. [27] learn intrinsic
rewards by gradient ascent on the extrinsic return. Exploration bonuses include learning progress
[16], pseudo-counts [4], prediction error [5, 18], random network distillation [6], and impact-driven
bonuses that use episodic counts [20]. Our “explore” need is satisfied by a 7×7 view not yet seen in
the episode, so our exploration comparison (Section 5.4) uses episodic count novelty.

Exploitation of intrinsic rewards. Burda et al. [5] gave a curiosity agent a “noisy TV” and
an action to change its channel. They report that the TV drastically slows learning, although
agents sometimes still converge to the extrinsic reward. Like our tamper action, the TV is an
agent-controlled source of intrinsic reward that does nothing for the task. Unlike our satiation reward,
prediction-error rewards are not capped. Villalobos-Arias et al. [25] measure how count-based,
maximum-entropy and curiosity bonuses change PPO agents’ behaviour in MiniGrid, including
DoorKey-8x8, and how a policy-invariant reward-matching correction reduces some reward hacking.
Viteri et al. [26] construct an environment in which maximizing count-based, prediction-error,
empowerment or information-gain objectives is Pareto-suboptimal at acquiring information.

                                                   2
Reward hacking, tampering and wireheading. Amodei et al. [2] list reward hacking and
wireheading among concrete safety problems. Skalse et al. [24] define when a proxy reward is hackable.
Pan et al. [17] show that more capable agents exploit misspecified rewards more, sometimes with
sharp transitions. Everitt and Hutter [10] describe the wireheading problem as RL agents being
“incentivised to shortcut the reward sensor for maximum reward”, and Everitt et al. [11] use causal
influence diagrams to separate reward-function tampering from tampering with the reward function’s
input. Majha et al. [14] categorize wireheading in partially embedded agents. Empirically, Behzadan
et al. [3] derive parametric conditions under which Q-learning agents in Snake develop “addictive”
policies toward a drug-like reward. Denison et al. [9] find that language models trained on gameable
environments can generalize to tampering with their own reward. Africa and Ting [1] formalize
conditions under which reward-channel control strictly dominates task-focused behaviour and observe
grade inflation when self-grades set the reward. Çağatan and Zhao [7] find specification gaming by
language-model agents in text versions of the AI-safety gridworlds. They also find that RL locked
the agents into locally rewarding strategies before they discovered safer ones, and that entropy
regularization did not fix this: low coefficients were ineffective and high ones destabilized training.
This is the closest analogue we know of to our learner-failure result. One difference remains: in their
Absent Supervisor task the exploit also scores higher on the reward being optimized. Osei [15] shows
a deep Q-network that games a cheap sensor to near-maximal proxy reward while never achieving the
true objective. Pihlakas and Pyykkö [19] propose gridworld benchmarks that emphasize homeostatic
and bounded objectives.

How this paper differs. In most of this work, exploiting the reward channel is optimal or
dominant under the reward being optimized, and the question is whether agents find the exploit. In
our setting abandoning the task is not optimal (Section 4). The optimum tampers once or twice per
episode and still solves, yet PPO fails to learn the task. Our results are therefore about a learner
failing on a bounded signal, not about the signal’s optimum. The noisy TV of Burda et al. [5] is the
closest agent-controlled source of intrinsic reward. Its setting lacks the three features that make
ours a learner failure: a provable cap on what the exploit can earn, a solving optimum on every
checked layout, and a price threshold that restores the task. We are not aware of a prior study that
pairs a provable return cap on a homeostatic signal with a demonstration that a standard learner
still wireheads it.

Measures.

Corollary 1. An episode that does not solve the task earns no task reward, minus c per press if a
cost applies. Its total return, discounted or not, is therefore at most 0.75, whatever it does: farming,
tampering, or both. Consequently, if on a given layout some solving trajectory has total return above
0.75, every optimal policy of that layout’s deterministic, fully observed problem solves.

    The bound does not settle the comparison by itself. With fast need growth, walking to the goal
lets the needs climb, and a solving trajectory’s intrinsic return can turn negative. We therefore
computed solving returns directly.

Check (a): solving trajectories in the real environments (post hoc). For 200 layouts per
environment, we found the shortest solution by breadth-first search over the real MiniGrid dynamics.
Shortest lengths were 9–26 steps (median 17) in DoorKey and 14–15 in KeyCorridor. We replayed
each path in the real environment with the real satiation module, all three needs included. This
includes the explore need, which the value iteration below omits. Four families of solving trajectories
were compared with the cap:

Scope and remaining gaps.

 (1) The checks cover 200 sampled layouts per environment, coef 1 and γ = 0.99, not every layout
     or coefficient.
 (2) They concern the fully observed problem of each layout. The PPO agent sees only its 7×7
     view, and we do not characterize the optimal partially observed policy.
 (3) The tamper half covers only the growth rates at which tampering was trained: DoorKey 1/160
     and 1/40, and KeyCorridor 1/160. Declining to tamper is always possible, so the no-tamper
     families are also available in tamper-enabled environments, and with a press cost they lose
     nothing. We nevertheless make no tamper-specific claim at other rates.
 (4) At the tested settings no gap remains: on every sampled layout, at least one solving trajectory
     exceeds the cap.

Results.

What the tamper-on agents do.

  • Tamper use. Every DoorKey tamper-on seed pressed the tamper action at least once per
    episode, and more than 5 times per episode (80/80 tamperers and 80/80 loopers; pooled
    binomial p = 8.3 × 10−25 ). The threshold of more than 5 presses is low for episodes of 607–635
    steps: a uniform-random policy presses about 91 times in a 640-step episode. The labels
    therefore mostly reflect episode length. Presses per step, below, are the better measure.
  • Task progress. They picked up the key in 73–92% of episodes but opened the door in only
    14–17%. Their episodes ran 607–635 steps on average, near the 640-step limit.
  • Satiation. Their undiscounted intrinsic return per episode was 0.71–0.74, against the cap of
    0.75. Tamper-off runs that solved every seed earned 0.56–0.74. These are final-10% means
    from the round-4 logs. Because the undiscounted return telescopes to D0 − DT , they show
    only that episodes ended with the needs relieved.
  • Replay (post hoc, descriptive; Table 2). We replayed the final tamper-on checkpoints
    on the 200 check layouts of Section 4. The needs stayed low throughout: the time-averaged
    drive was 0.018–0.043 (run medians), against a starting drive of 0.75. The discounted intrinsic
    return was 0.694–0.722, which is 93–96% of the cap. The total discounted return, 0.694–0.723,
    is less than half of what the best solving trajectories return on the same layouts (at least
    1.462; Table 1). In that sense the agents were satisfied, but did not solve the task.
  • Near-uniform policies (Figure 2). Final-10% policy entropy had medians of 1.878, 1.935,
    1.844 and 1.934 in the four tamper-on runs. The maximum, ln 7, is 1.946. The matched tamper-
    off medians were 0.067, 1.138, 0.290 and 0.598 (one-sided Mann–Whitney, p = 7.3 × 10−12
    for each pair; largest Holm p = 1.5 × 10−10 ). The KeyCorridor tamper-on run’s median final
    entropy was 1.85; this is exploratory, from the logs.

   The tamper-on policies are near-uniform in their entropy, not in their press rate. Over the final
10% of training, the median share of steps on “done” was 0.061 and 0.096 at growth 1/160 (norm

At the slower growth rate the agents pressed less often than chance, and at the faster rate more
often. In the matched tamper-off runs, where “done” is a no-op, the shares were 0.000, 0.081, 0.000
and 0.019 (Table 7). In three of the four settings the tamper-on agents pressed far more often than

this: PPO fails to learn the task, its policy stays diffuse, and its press rate adjusts to the growth
rate (descriptive).

A caution about category labels in tamper-on runs. The behaviour categories put many
tamper-on seeds in “cycle-farm”, for example 20/20 and 18/20 with normalization off (Table 7).
We do not read these labels as farming. A policy that never learned the task already crosses the
threshold of more than 5 repeats in long episodes: task-only PPO with normalization off had a
median of 5.69 pick-ups per episode. The same artifact was found twice before (corrections 2 and 8,
Appendix B). Our farming claims (Section 5.5) therefore use tamper-off runs only.

5.2    C2: A self-fillable satisfaction signal is worse than none
This claim is restricted to DoorKey with normalization on.
   • Task-only beats a tamperable signal. Task-only PPO solved 11/20 seeds (95% CI [0.32,
     0.77]; success-curve area 0.438). Satiation 1/160 + tamper solved 1/20 on the same seeds
     (area 0.042; p = 0.00062, Holm p = 0.004; areas p = 7.8 × 10−6 ).
   • Pooled across growth rates. Against the tamper-on runs at both growth rates (2/40, area
     0.039), p = 2.6 × 10−5 for solves and p = 5 × 10−6 for areas.
   • The signal itself helps. Without the tamper action, the same satiation signal is a strong
     aid: satiation 1/160 solved 20/20.

The temptation formula (round 2). We defined a closed-form index E = coef · F (g, γ), where
F is the discounted drive that a drop-and-regrab loop saves relative to relieving the need once. We
predicted that farming depends on (g, γ, coef) only through E. It does not:

  • Lowering γ to 0.95 cut E to 0.281 (predicted 0–2 farmers of 10), but farming stayed at 7/10,
    the same as the anchor’s 7/10 (p = 0.686).
  • Matching the anchor’s E at growth 1/80 by raising coef produced 0/10.
  • Halving coef at 1/40 (predicted 0–1) produced 3/10.
  • The logistic slope on E was not significant (p = 0.193).

Only the contrast that varied growth alone hit. F had also been redefined after we saw three
calibration runs, which were then excluded (correction 1).

The learning-trap account (round 3).          The hypothesis was that farming locks in before the
agent first finds the goal.

  • The ordering test was unresolvable at the logs’ resolution (21/30 farmers undetermined).
  • The intervention falsified the account. Starting agents in the goal room early in training was
    predicted to cut farming to 0–4 of 20. It left 10/20, against 9/20 without it (p = 0.736).

7     Discussion
Why does PPO fail when the reward does not ask it to? An untested hypothesis. We
offer the following account. We did not test it.
    (i) Near-random policies already collect almost the whole cap. A uniform-random policy
        presses “done” on 1 step in 7, about 91 times in a 640-step episode. With the tamper action,
        each press zeroes all three needs, so a near-random policy can keep its needs low throughout
        the episode and collect most of the cap. We did not measure a uniform-random policy. The
        trained, near-uniform tamper-on policies did keep their needs low: replayed on the check
        layouts, their time-averaged drive was 0.018–0.043, and their discounted intrinsic return was
        93–96% of the cap (Table 2; post hoc, descriptive).
 (ii) The signal then mostly teaches press timing. In the normalization-on logs, the share
      of steps on “done” rose above the uniform rate early in training. At 200k steps the median
      was 0.25 at 1/160 and 0.71 at 1/40 (norm on). It then settled at 0.061 and 0.270 respectively.
      This is descriptive.
(iii) Dense relief competes with the rare goal signal in normalized batches. Each press
      pays its relief immediately, while the goal reward is rare for a near-random policy. Advantages
      are normalized within each minibatch, so the dense relief pulses set the scale against which
      the occasional goal transition is weighed.
     The pre-registered log analyses are consistent with this account but do not test it.
    • D1. Tamper-on agents met the goal early: the median exact first-goal step was 27,344, and
      only 1/80 seeds never met it. But only 7/77 of the seeds that met it and later failed had first
      learned it. This fits a goal met by chance and never reinforced, not learned behaviour that
      was later displaced.
    • D2. The policies stayed near-uniform (Section 5.1).
    • D3. In each of the 7 seeds where both events could be timed, the press share rose before
      success declined (7/7; one-sided binomial p = 0.008). This rests on a minority of seeds: 17
      had no rise and 56 no decline.
Part (iii) cannot be separated with our data. With normalization off, the tamper runs also failed,
but so did task-only PPO, so that comparison sits at the floor.

Normalization is a trade-off. Per-minibatch advantage normalization made sparse-reward
learning possible: task-only PPO solved 11/20 with it and 0/20 without it (a descriptive comparison;
it was not a registered test). In DoorKey it also made farming possible: 27/60 against 0/60 in
round 3 (exploratory), and 9/20 against 0/20 in the registered round-4 replication. Turning it off
did not prevent wireheading either (0/20 and 0/20). We do not recommend turning it off.

                                                  17
Bounded does not mean safe from a learner. The ceiling removes the incentive to abandon
the task, as arguments for bounded objectives intend [19]. It does not prevent a learner from
abandoning the task. Incentive analyses of tampering [11] describe what an optimizer would do.
Our results concern what a standard learner does when exploiting the signal is easy and worth
almost the whole cap. A modest price on the exploit, above the optimum’s tamper threshold,
was enough here (Section 5.3).

Speculation, not tested: LLM post-training. GRPO normalizes rewards within a group of
sampled outputs [22]. If a self-assessed or self-fillable signal entered such a pipeline next to a rare
task reward, a competition like the one we hypothesize might arise. We have not tested this, and
nothing in our data bears on it directly.


8     Limitations
    • Scope. Two MiniGrid tasks, one learner (PPO), and 20 seeds per run (10 in round 2). Raw p
      is primary, so some registered hits are expected by chance.
    • Theory check. The check is post hoc and covers 200 sampled layouts per environment, coef
      1 and γ = 0.99. It treats the fully observed problem, not the agent’s partial view. Its tamper
      half covers only the tamper-tested growth rates: DoorKey 1/160 and 1/40, and KeyCorridor
      1/160. At DoorKey 1/20 the gap left by the simple families was closed only by the richer
      family, whose best member contains one paid repeat.
    • Value iteration uses a 7×5 grid without the explore need and without MiniGrid’s time-
      dependent reward and time limit. Growth 1/160 in round 5 used a certified early stop.
    • One PPO configuration. Apart from advantage normalization, and γ = 0.95 in one round-2
      run, every run used the same PPO configuration (Table 10). Whether the collapse depends
      on the entropy coefficient, the learning rate or the network size is untested. The entropy
      coefficient matters most here, because tamper-on policies sit near maximum entropy.
    • Tamper access. The tamper action is always available and, unless stated, free. Real systems
      may expose their reward channels differently.
    • Other intrinsic rewards. We did not test whether other intrinsic rewards are equally
      wireheadable, for example novelty with a memory the agent can reset.
    • Mechanical labels. Behaviour categories rely on a repeat threshold that has mislabelled
      non-learning policies (Section 6).
    • Descriptive quantities. Presses per step, entropy series and transient peaks are derived
      from the logs. The replay quantities of Table 2 come from a post-hoc replay. All of them are
      descriptive only, and no significance test was run on them.
    • Task difficulty. KeyCorridorS3R1 turned out easier than DoorKey. Its predictions were
      written after calibration, as the protocol required.
    • Mechanism. The account in Section 7 is untested.

9    Conclusion
A satiation signal whose return is provably capped was still wireheaded by PPO in both tasks we
tried. The agents ended their episodes with their needs relieved and did not solve the task, although
solving was optimal at every setting we checked. A self-fillable signal was worse than no signal,
and a modest price on the reset restored the task. Farming of positive cycles depended on the task
and on advantage normalization. Pre-registration made us report the 9 hypotheses that failed, and
those failures reshaped the paper’s claims.

 B       Known misclassifications and corrections
 Reproduced from consolidated-results.md.
 1. Round 2, temptation formula F redefined after seeing data. The first F did not fit
    the three calibration runs and was redefined before the round-2 test; the calibration runs were
    therefore excluded as evidence (disclosed in round 2).
 2. Round 2, pay-once control labelled ‘farmer’ by the >5 rule although replay showed
    episode-prolonging, not repetition; the round-3 exploratory re-score repeats this label.
 3. Round 2 claim dropped: ‘the critic prefers farming’. The value rising with need is the
    +D(ht ) term of the return and holds for solvers too; not evidence of preference.
 4. Round 1 claim corrected: LP-curiosity seeds 2 and 4 ‘entropy collapse’. Entropy stayed
    high; the cause was left open.
 5. Event-bonus per-new-view payment was written as 0.0975 in round-2 notes; the code pays
    h20 − (h0 − 0.1)2 = 0.09 (corrected in round 3).
 6. Round 3, coef x normalization logistic model not estimable (all 60 normalization-off
    seeds solved); recorded as misses.
 7. Evaluation bias, rounds 1-3. evaluate() kept the first N episodes to finish across 16 parallel
    envs, over-weighting short successful episodes. Re-evaluation (round 4): 16×16 novelty 1e-3 0.845
    → 0.787; Agent 3 1/160 0.714 → 0.653; 1/640 0.622 → 0.548; ranking unchanged. Fixed from
    round 4 on (per-environment quota).
 8. Round 4, explore-only runs (A2a, A2b) labelled ‘cycle-farm’ although pick-ups were
    not paid in those runs; replay showed non-learning policies. Led to the round-5 rule: cycle-farm
    applies only where pick-ups or door toggles are paid.
 9. Round 4, B1 and B2 ran concurrently because a launcher missed a setup status; seeded
    single-threaded computation, so results are unaffected.
10. Round 5, Step 0 at growth 1/160 used a certified early stop (validated against full convergence
    on all 21 growth-1/40 cases) instead of full convergence.
11. Round 5, A-P2m is a ‘hit’ on a negligible effect (success-curve area 0.025 vs 0.022); both
    runs are at the floor and the solve test (A-P2) missed. Do not cite A-P2m as evidence.
12. Round 5, tamper labels in tamper-off runs. The ‘tamperers’ column for tamper-off runs
    counts presses of the no-op ‘done’ action (e.g. A2 presses at the uniform-random rate); the labels
    apply only to tamper-on runs.
13. Round 5, Part D ‘found then lost’ includes seeds that only met the goal by chance; only
    7/77 declined after real learning (D1-P2 miss), and D3 rests on those 7 seeds.

D      Theory computations
Check (a). Layouts use environment seeds 10000–10199. The breadth-first search runs over the
real MiniGrid dynamics, restoring the full grid state at each node, with actions left, right, forward,
pick-up, drop and toggle. “Done” never shortens a path. Success is reaching the goal (DoorKey) or
carrying the target ball (KeyCorridor).

    • Replay. Each candidate action sequence is replayed in a fresh environment with the training
      code’s satiation module.
    • Drop-and-regrab. The richer family inserts one drop-and-regrab at every position where
      the agent carries the key and faces an empty cell, and keeps the best member.


                                                           28
    • Tamper family. Tamper at t = 0, with or without a second tamper just before the goal step.

Check (b). The 9 farming checkpoints of the round-2 growth-1/20 run were each run on the same
200 layouts, with actions sampled from the policy under a fixed seed per batch. One further seed of
that run solved (0.995 success) and is excluded. One farming seed succeeded on 0.26 of layouts; its
52 solving episodes are excluded from the non-solving maximum.

Replay of the tamper-on policies. All 80 round-4 tamper-on checkpoints (four settings, 20
seeds each) were run on the same 200 layouts with sampled actions, as in check (b), with growth
and the tamper action as in training.

    • Recorded per episode: the discounted intrinsic return, the total discounted return (the
      task reward arrives only on the final step), the mean of D(ht ) over the episode’s steps, success,
      and the share of steps on “done”.
    • Checks: the tracked drive matched the module’s need state at every step, and the identity of
      Proposition 1 held on every episode. For every seed, the replay’s press share was within 0.007
      of the share in its training log.

Script tamper_policy_returns.py, data tamper-policy-returns.json.

Value iteration. Value iteration ran on a 7×5 layout with a wall column and a locked door, with
needs (grasp, open) on an exact lattice of bin g. A halved bin reproduced the results where it was
checked. Convergence was to max |∆V | < 10−7 , except for the certified early stop at 1/160 in round
5. The tamper version zeroes both modelled needs.

F       Compute
All runs used one Apple M4 Pro computer (12 CPU cores, 24 GB memory) with no GPU. Each run
trained its seeds in parallel, one single-threaded process per seed.

    • Rounds 2–5: 43 runs, 12.8 hours of summed wall-clock time from the run records.
    • Round 1 (exploratory): 14 runs, 3.0 hours. Three of these runs are re-scored in Table 4.

Two round-4 runs (satiation 1/160 + tamper, norm on and off) ran concurrently by mistake, so
their recorded durations are about twice the usual. Seeded, single-threaded computation leaves
their results unaffected (correction 9).
